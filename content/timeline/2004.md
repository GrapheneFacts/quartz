**Evidence:** Well-supported

## The experiment, without the myth layer

Andre Geim, Konstantin Novoselov, and collaborators isolate few-layer and monolayer graphene by mechanical cleavage from graphite — the “Scotch tape” method — transfer flakes onto oxidized silicon, and measure an electric-field effect in atomically thin carbon films.

**Landmark:** Novoselov et al., *Science* **306**, 666 (2004), “Electric field effect in atomically thin carbon films.”

What made 2004 the inflection point was the *combination*:

1. Flakes you could see (contrast on SiO₂ of the right thickness).
2. A monolayer assignment that the community accepted.
3. A device geometry (gate through the oxide) that produced a field effect.
4. Numbers that lined up with the 2D honeycomb / Dirac picture Wallace had sketched in [[timeline/1947]].

Chemists had already made very thin carbon ([[timeline/1962]]). Surface scientists had already grown monolayer carbon on metals and SiC ([[timeline/1963-2003]]). 2004 is when a freely handled sheet on an insulator became an *electronic material other people could copy next week*.

## Culture note, kept in its box

Manchester lore ties this to “Friday night experiments” — side bets next to official projects. Useful color. Not a substitute for the *Science* paper.

## What 2004 did not do

- Did not invent carbon sheets.
- Did not produce square metres of CVD graphene (that scale-up is later).
- Did not settle quality standards for powders sold as graphene in 2026.
- Did not imply graphene would replace silicon CMOS. Band gap, contacts, and wafer uniformity showed up immediately as engineering walls ([[timeline/2011-2012]]).
